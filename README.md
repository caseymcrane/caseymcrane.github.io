## Research

I use equivariant degree theory to study existence and global bifurcation problems in dynamical systems featuring high degrees of symmetry and time delays. Using these methods, I classify bifurcating branches of solutions by their symmetries, show their global continuation, and identify solutions with prescribed symmetry types. I have a particular interest in applying these techniques in practically relevant settings where classical bifurcation theories are difficult to apply, and where the bifurcation of solutions has interesting and useful interpretations. For this reason, my recent focus has been on homogeneous multi-agent systems, where networks of agents with identical decision-making protocols achieve a stable consensus. When a parameter of interest is pushed beyond a critical threshold, this stable consensus can break down, causing the agent population to fragment into opposing clusters according to the symmetries of their interaction topology. When this bifurcation leads to periodic solutions, this can form a periodic multiconsensus state, where different clusters oscillate out of phase. 

I have studied this consensus-breaking bifurcation in a variety of multi-agent systems featuring time lags and memory. In my doctoral thesis, "Consensus-Breaking Global Hopf Bifurcation in Memory-Based Multi-Agent Systems", I show how the weight of an agent's memory of its own past states can cause consensus-breaking bifurcations in a variety of systems with real-world applications. In a model of UAVs autonomously maintaining a geometric formation, where each UAV adjusts its position based on its deviation from a set point and its neighbors, a UAV's continuous memory of its own past displacements can cause stable oscillations around formation set points, tunable in frequency and amplitude. This construction can be read as a finite-horizon anti-windup modification of a PID formation controller: truncating the integrator prevents windup, at the cost of permitting periodic oscillations whose onset we characterize exactly.

In a model of asset markets featuring two distinct populations of traders linked through the larger global economy, I show how fundamentalist traders—who buy or sell based on a moving average of an asset's market price—can combine with chartist traders (who extrapolate recent price trends) or momentum traders to cause price bubbles and crashes. In related work—introduced in my thesis and currently in revision at SIADS—I also identified a class of delay equations I call pseudoneutral: equations written in the implicit form of neutral equations, but are in fact of retarded type, and that arise naturally as the mean-field limit of populations with distributed neutral delays. This distinction clarifies which instabilities are genuinely neutral and which are artefacts of a single, exactly synchronized lag.

A recurring theme throughout this dynamical systems work is a hybrid approach: using equivariant topological methods to obtain powerful abstract results on the symmetries and global continuation of bifurcating branches, then using these topological guarantees as an anchor for numerical simulations and analysis to obtain information which topological methods alone cannot give, such as the stability or minimal period of the detected branches. This combined approach leverages the strengths of each method and compensates the weaknesses of the other, and I believe it is an ideal toolkit for many applied problems. 

Alongside consensus dynamics, I study memory in other models of strategic interaction between agents. In "Trust as hysteresis: analytically tractable strategies for the iterated prisoner's dilemma" (under review at Journal of Theoretical Biology, preprint available), I introduce a family of trust-based strategies for the iterated prisoner's dilemma in which trust is modeled by a hysteron—an elementary hysteresis operator—so that it responds to an accumulated history of interactions rather than to the most recent round alone. A geometric framework reduces the analysis to affine contractions of the unit square, yielding exact characterizations of the cost of betrayal and of the patterns of defection that can be sustained indefinitely without breaking trust, which turn out to be exactly the lower Christoffel words. Both strands concern history-dependent systems whose behavior is mediated by threshold events—delay equations in which the past enters continuously, and hysteretic strategic models in which discrete threshold crossings select the agent's response.
